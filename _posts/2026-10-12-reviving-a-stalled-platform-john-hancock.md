In September 2022 I moved to Boston to work on-site for John Hancock as Release Train Engineer and Technical Product Manager. The programme I joined had been held up for two years.

COVID had put the platform on hold. When work restarted, the backlog was still there, only larger. More than 400 tickets were waiting, and the roadmap had broken into pieces that no longer fitted together.

## Where do you even start?

Not with the tickets. With the way work moved through the system.

My starting point was release governance: how work was planned, sequenced and released. A backlog that size is not a to-do list; it's a symptom. If the release process can't absorb work, adding more people only adds more queue.

Then capacity. I applied Function Point capacity models so we planned against what the teams could really deliver, not what we hoped they could. That turned a fragmented roadmap into sprint-ready schedules people could commit to.

## Scaling once it moved

Once the train was moving, the business wanted more, and rightly so. We coached teams through coordinated upgrades so that **five modernisation initiatives could run at the same time across more than 20 business divisions**. That only works when dependencies are visible and release dates mean something.

I presented release roadmaps to executive leadership and kept runbooks current, because people change on long programmes. When they did, nothing fell through the gaps.

## The outcome

- **400+ tickets cleared in 9 months** on a programme that had been stalled for two years.
- **Five modernisation initiatives** running concurrently across **20+ divisions**.
- **Zero delivery gaps** through team transitions.

## What I learned in Boston

A stalled programme is rarely a people problem. It's usually a flow problem that has turned into a morale problem. Fix the flow, make progress visible, and people start believing in the plan again. That belief is what really clears a backlog.
