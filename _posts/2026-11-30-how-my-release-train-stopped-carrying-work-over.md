In September 2025 I took over a release train for a global insurer. The teams were good. The plan wasn't holding.

Every iteration (sprint), a few stories slid into the next one. By the end of a Planning Interval (PI), that slide had become carry-over: work we had committed to, moving into the next PI. And once a plan starts slipping, the business starts adding changes on top of it, because the plan no longer feels fixed.

## Why the work kept slipping

When I looked at what was spilling, it came back to three gaps.

First, dependencies between teams weren't discussed before the work started. A team would begin a story and only then find it was waiting on another team.

Second, planning and estimation weren't tied to the budget. We agreed on what to build without checking it against the hours the budget actually allowed.

Third, the business hadn't confirmed that the scope was final. Without that, every new idea could still come in mid-PI.

## Dependencies go on the board at PI planning

We now map dependencies during PI planning itself, on the ART planning board. Each team pins its features to the iteration it plans to deliver them in, and a string links every feature to the work it's waiting on from another team. A string that crosses into a later iteration is a warning we can see on the wall. If a dependency can't be met inside the PI, the feature doesn't go into the commitment.

## Estimate in hours, against the budget

In the same session we estimate the work in hours and check it against the budget. That turns "can we do this?" into a number everyone can see. When the hours don't fit, we cut or move scope there and then, while it's cheap.

Then every team votes on its confidence in the plan, fist of five. A low vote means we talk it through and fix the plan before anyone commits.

## The business confirms the end result

After PI planning, the business analyst holds a series of calls with the business. The goal is simple: agree on the end result they expect, within the budget, before the teams start. Only then is the scope final.

## What changed

Scope changes stopped arriving in the middle of the PI. Stories stopped spilling from one iteration to the next. And the train now finishes each PI with no committed work carried over.

Across my time on the train, ART predictability has averaged 95%+, measured the SAFe way: business value delivered against business value planned for each PI. Sprint variance stays under 5%.

## What I learned

There's no fixed number of those calls with the business. We decide based on what each PI needs. Some settle quickly, others take longer.

What doesn't change is the order. No commitment until the dependencies are mapped, the hours fit the budget and the business has said yes to the end result. Most carry-over isn't a delivery problem. It's a planning problem that shows up late.
