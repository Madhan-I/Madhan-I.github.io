Most of my career has been remote or split across time zones. Teams in Chennai and clients in the US, daily stand-ups over video, decisions in chat threads. It works, and I'm good at it.

Then, while I was on-site in Boston for John Hancock, I started going to the in-person Code for Boston nights at the Cambridge Innovation Center.

## The building

CIC sits at One Broadway in Kendall Square, a short walk from MIT. It was founded in 1999 by two MIT graduates as shared space for start-ups, and it has housed hundreds of young companies since; HubSpot was a tenant in its early years. On a Tuesday evening the café floor fills with people who have finished their day jobs and stayed for something they care about.

It became my regular stop. Besides Code for Boston, I went to the Venture Café Thursday Gatherings in the same building, Boston Code Camp at Microsoft's NERD Center, and free public technical lectures at MIT and Harvard. Code for Boston met at CIC once or twice a month. The other weeks it met on Zoom. Same people, same projects, same agenda. The difference between those two kinds of evening is what stayed with me.

## What changed in the room

**Problems got smaller faster.** On Zoom, a blocked volunteer often stays quiet until the end. In the room you can see someone stuck, pull up a chair and sort it out in minutes.

**Newcomers stayed.** Someone who walked in, got a welcome and a task, and sat next to a regular usually came back. Online, people were far easier to lose after their first visit.

**Cross-team questions got asked.** The data, backend and frontend groups sat at neighboring tables, so "what does your API actually return?" was a walk across the room, not a ticket.

**Trust built quickly.** One face-to-face conversation did more for working relationships than weeks of chat messages. Disagreements on later video calls were easier, because people knew each other.

## What I took back to distributed delivery

I'm not arguing everyone should be in an office. I've delivered well across onshore and offshore teams for years. But those evenings changed how I plan.

- **Spend face time on the hard moments.** PI planning, a program restart or a new team forming is where an in-person session pays for itself many times over.
- **Design onboarding for people who aren't in the room.** If a new person only does well when someone sits next to them, the onboarding needs fixing.
- **Make being stuck visible on purpose.** Remote teams need an easy, blame-free way to say "I'm blocked", because nobody can see it on their face.
- **Build relationships before you need them.** The time to get to know a stakeholder is before the difficult release, not during it.

Those nights in Kendall Square were volunteer evenings, not work. They still taught me some of the most useful things I know about running teams that are spread across the world.
